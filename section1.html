睡眠解寤，宜欢喜思，听我所说，撰记佛言。所行非常，谓兴衰法，夫生辄死，此灭为乐。譬如陶家，埏埴作器，一切要坏，人命亦然。如河驶流，往而不返，人命如是，逝者不还。譬人操杖，行牧食牛，老死犹然，亦养命去。千百非一，族姓男女，贮聚财产，无不衰丧。生者日夜，命自攻削，寿之消尽，如漏水然。常者皆尽，高者亦堕，合会有离，生者有死。众生相克，以丧其命，随行所堕，自受殃福。老见苦痛，死则意去，乐家缚狱，贪世不断。咄嗟老至，色变作耄，少时如意，老见蹈藉。虽寿百岁，亦死过去，为老所厌，病条至际。是日已过，命则随减，如少水鱼，斯有何乐？老则色衰，所病自坏，形败腐朽，命终自然。是身何用？恒漏臭处，为病所困，有老死患。嗜欲自恣，非法是增，不见闻变，寿命无常。非有子恃，亦非父兄，为死所迫，无亲可怙。昼夜慢惰，老不止淫，有财不施，不受佛言。有此四弊，为自侵欺。非空非海中，非入山石间，无有地方所，脱之不受死。是务是吾作，当作令致是，人为此喧扰，履践老死忧。

逐句解释

睡眠解寤，宜欢喜思，听我所说，撰记佛言。

从沉睡中醒来，应以欢喜之心思惟；请听我宣说，谨记佛陀的教言。

Awake from sleep, rejoice in thought; hear what I declare — these words the Buddha spoke.

The chapter opens as a summons to wakefulness, both literal and spiritual. To hear the Dharma one must first be awake — not sleepwalking through life on habit and assumption.

所行非常，谓兴衰法，夫生辄死，此灭为乐。

一切所行之法皆是无常，兴盛与衰败乃其本质；有生必有死，唯有寂灭才是真正的安乐。

All formations are impermanent — this is the law of rise and fall. What is born must die; their cessation is true peace.

This verse states the first truth of Buddhism with stark economy: everything conditioned arises and passes. The cessation the verse points to is Nibbana — not annihilation but the end of the compulsive cycle of arising.

譬如陶家，埏埴作器，一切要坏，人命亦然。

犹如陶工用泥土塑造器皿，所有器皿终究会破损；人的生命也是如此，终将毁坏消逝。

Just as a potter shapes vessels from clay — all must break in the end — so too is human life.

The potter's wheel is one of the oldest metaphors for impermanence in world literature. The clay pot is beautiful, useful, valued — and fragile. The teaching is not to stop making pots but to hold them without illusion.

如河驶流，往而不返，人命如是，逝者不还。

如同江河奔流而去，一去不复返，人的生命也是如此，逝去的光阴和生命，永不回头。

Like a river rushing on, never turning back — human life is the same; what has passed does not return.

The river image makes impermanence visible and kinetic. We can see the water flowing — we cannot see it stop or reverse. Life is that river. The instruction is not to grieve the flow but to understand it.

譬人操杖，行牧食牛，老死犹然，亦养命去。

就像牧人持杖驱赶牛群去觅食一样，老与死也在驱赶着我们的生命，时光一天天地消逝。

Like a herdsman with his staff driving cattle to pasture — so too do aging and death drive life away, day by day.

Old age and death are here cast as herdsmen: patient, methodical, unstoppable. We are the cattle. The image punctures any illusion of control — the herd goes where the herdsman directs, whether it wishes to or not.

千百非一，族姓男女，贮聚财产，无不衰丧。

无论是来自千百个家族的男男女女，无论积累了多少财富，没有一个不会衰败消亡。

Not one in hundreds or thousands — men and women of every clan — who stores up wealth does not in the end decay and perish.

The universal scope is deliberate: not some people, not most people — none. Accumulated wealth provides comfort and security in its time, but no one has ever negotiated their way out of aging and death.

生者日夜，命自攻削，寿之消尽，如漏水然。

生命每日每夜都在自我削减，寿命悄悄消耗殆尽，就像容器里的水不断漏失一样。

Day and night the living diminish; life erodes itself; the span of years drains away like water from a leaking vessel.

The leaking vessel is an image of slow, continuous, inevitable loss. No dramatic catastrophe — just the steady drip that empties what once seemed full. Each day is already gone as it passes.

常者皆尽，高者亦堕，合会有离，生者有死。

长久的终将结束，高处的终将跌落，相聚终将离散，有生命的终将死去。

Whatever is lasting will exhaust itself; whatever is high will fall; whatever is joined will part; whatever lives will die.

Four universal laws stated in four compact clauses. Together they cover every dimension in which humans seek permanence — time, status, relationship, life itself. None holds.

众生相克，以丧其命，随行所堕，自受殃福。

众生相互竞争，因此丧失生命；随着各自的行为而堕落或上升，自己承受祸与福的果报。

Living beings consume one another, losing their lives; each falls according to its deeds and bears its own weal or woe.

The chain of cause and consequence — karma — is stated plainly: beings create the conditions of their own experience through their actions. The suffering is not arbitrary punishment; it is the natural echo of one's own conduct.

老见苦痛，死则意去，乐家缚狱，贪世不断。

年老时受尽苦痛，死亡时意识离散，贪恋家园如被囚于牢狱，对世间的贪著永无止息。

Old age brings pain to witness; death takes the mind away; attachment to the household binds like a prison; craving for the world never ceases.

The verse sketches the full arc of unawakened life: suffering builds through aging, ends in the confusion of death, and is sustained throughout by two forms of attachment — to home/family and to the world generally. The 'prison' image is sharp: we built the cage ourselves, from love.

咄嗟老至，色变作耄，少时如意，老见蹈藉。

叹息着老年悄然而至，容颜变得苍老昏耄，年少时随心所欲，年老时却处处被人漠视踩践。

Alas, old age arrives — form turns grey and frail; youth had its way, but old age is trampled upon.

The word 咄嗟 (alas) carries a note of lamentation. The contrast is social as much as physical: the young person commands respect and capability, while the old person is pushed aside. The verse does not sentimentalize aging; it describes it plainly.

虽寿百岁，亦死过去，为老所厌，病条至际。

即使活到百岁，也终究会死去；被衰老所折磨，疾病一条条地步步逼近。

Though one lives a hundred years, death still claims him; wearied by old age, illness advances step by step to the end.

A hundred years was a proverbially long life in the ancient world — and even that is not enough. The verse resists any notion that living long solves the problem of impermanence. Length of life and quality of understanding are separate matters.

是日已过，命则随减，如少水鱼，斯有何乐？

今日已过，生命随之减少一分，如同浅水中的鱼，处境危急，这样的生命有什么可快乐的？

This day has passed — life is diminished by it. Like a fish in dwindling water — what joy is there in this?

The fish in dwindling water is one of the most urgent images in the chapter. There is no gradual adjustment — the water simply gets shallower every day. The fish has nowhere to go. The question is rhetorical but not despairing: its purpose is to wake us up, not defeat us.

老则色衰，所病自坏，形败腐朽，命终自然。

年老则形貌衰颓，疾病使身体自行败坏，肉身最终腐朽，生命自然走向终结。

Old age dims the form; disease ruins the body from within; flesh decays and crumbles; life ends as a matter of course.

The verse is clinical rather than morbid — describing the body's process without either denying it or dwelling on it with horror. This unflinching clarity is itself the teaching: know this body for what it is, and do not organize your life around its maintenance alone.

是身何用？恒漏臭处，为病所困，有老死患。

这个身体有什么值得执著的？它永远在漏泄污秽，被疾病所困，充满了老与死的隐患。

What use is this body? — ever leaking, foul-smelling, plagued by sickness, beset by aging and death.

The rhetorical question is a meditation prompt: look at the body honestly. The teaching is not self-hatred but clear-eyed seeing. A body seen as it actually is — impermanent, prone to disease, inevitably aging — loses its grip as the primary object of identity and concern.

嗜欲自恣，非法是增，不见闻变，寿命无常。

纵情于欲望，不善之法因此增长；不去学习和观察无常的变化，却忘记了寿命本是无常的。

Indulging every craving, one grows in wrong conduct; failing to see and hear how things change, one forgets that life itself is impermanent.

Sensory indulgence and failure to attend to impermanence feed each other. The person who does not reflect on change naturally becomes absorbed in the pursuit of pleasure — and that absorption then deepens the avoidance of reality. Breaking the cycle requires a deliberate turning of attention.

非有子恃，亦非父兄，为死所迫，无亲可怙。

面对死亡，没有儿子可以依靠，父兄也无济于事；死亡逼临之时，再没有亲人能够庇护你。

No child can protect you, no father or brother; when death presses close, there is no kinsman to shelter you.

Every culture instinctively turns to family in the face of the ultimate. This verse strips away that consolation — not cruelly, but honestly. The work of facing mortality is ultimately individual. Others can accompany us; they cannot do it for us.

昼夜慢惰，老不止淫，有财不施，不受佛言。

日夜懈怠懒惰，年老仍不止欲，有财富而不布施，不接受佛陀的教言。

Slothful by day and night; not checking sensual desire even in old age; hoarding wealth without giving; refusing the Buddha's word —

The verse lists four failures that compound one another. Laziness prevents practice; unchecked desire in old age shows no learning from life's lessons; hoarding contracts the self; and rejecting the teaching closes the only door. The next verse delivers the conclusion.

有此四弊，为自侵欺。

有这四种弊病，就是在自我伤害、自我欺骗。

One who has these four faults harms and deceives themselves alone.

The weight falls on 'themselves.' There is no other victim here — the person who refuses to practise, who stays bound by sensuality, who hoards and rejects guidance, injures no one but the one who bears the consequences. Self-deception is the deepest form of this harm.

非空非海中，非入山石间，无有地方所，脱之不受死。

不论在空中、在海中，还是深入山石之间，天下没有任何一个地方，能让人逃脱死亡的降临。

Not in the sky, not in the ocean's depths, not by entering mountain caves — nowhere in the world is there a place where one can escape death.

The verse surveys every domain humans might imagine as a refuge — sky, sea, mountain — and dismisses each. The point is not geographical but psychological: there is no escape through avoidance or hiding. The only genuine response to death is understanding its nature.

是务是吾作，当作令致是，人为此喧扰，履践老死忧。

人们总说'这是我要做的，这件事做完了再做那件事'，终日在这样的喧扰中，踏上了老与死的忧患之路。

"This I must do; that I must accomplish" — in this clamour human beings tread the path of aging and sorrow.

The verse captures ordinary busyness with wry precision: the endless list of tasks and obligations that fills a life, while the real question — how to meet aging and death — goes unaddressed. We are occupied with everything except what matters most.

总结 · Summary

无常品以二十一颂揭示了生命的根本处境：万物无常，有生必灭，财富、家族、权势都无法抗拒老死的来临。经文用陶器、流河、漏水等生动比喻，反复敲击同一个真相：执著于无常之物，是人生一切苦恼的根源。唯有清醒地看见无常，才能走上通往真正解脱的道路。

The chapter on Impermanence opens the Dhammapada with its most foundational teaching: nothing conditioned lasts. Through twenty-one verses employing images of rivers, potter's clay, leaking vessels, fish in dwindling water, and the herdsman of old age, the text relentlessly demonstrates that wealth, family, youth, and life itself offer no permanent refuge. The chapter's purpose is not despair but clarity — seeing impermanence honestly is the precondition for all genuine practice.
